1. No Power Is Absolute

Legitimate power is power whose limits are defined and whose exercise is subject to effective oversight.

Document ID: nazm-dad:p1:v1 | Version: 1.0 | Last revised: 2026-08-31

1-1. Legal Competence Must Be Defined Before Public Power Is Exercised §

No public institution should be able to determine or expand the scope of its own authority solely on the basis of its own will.

The legal competence of each institution must be established on the basis of general and justiciable rules so that citizens, other institutions, and oversight bodies can determine which actions fall within lawful authority and which exceed it.

The limits of public power should be as clear, public, and predictable as reasonably possible.

1-2. Power Must Not Be the Final Judge of Its Own Limits §

If an institution can define its own limits, determine whether it has complied with those limits, and prevent meaningful external review, the limitation of power becomes ineffective in practice.

For this reason, the lawfulness of an exercise of public power must not be determined solely by the institution that exercised that power.

Independent review must be possible.

1-3. The Distribution of Legal Competences Is a Safeguard Against Concentrated Power §

The simultaneous concentration of decision-making, implementation, oversight, and adjudication in a single centre increases the risk of abuse of power.

The institutional order should therefore distribute important legal competences among different institutions so that no single institution controls every stage of decision-making and implementation without effective oversight.

The distribution of legal competences is not designed to create institutional paralysis. Its purpose is to prevent necessary authority from becoming unchecked power.

1-4. Oversight Must Be Real and Effective §

The mere existence of an oversight body on paper is not sufficient to constrain public power.

Effective oversight requires lawful access to relevant information, sufficient independence, authority to investigate, and the ability to act on established violations.

If an oversight institution cannot detect violations, question them, or make its findings practically effective, oversight becomes a procedural formality rather than a real safeguard.

1-5. The Exercise of Public Power Must Be Subject to Challenge §

Where a decision or action of a public institution affects the rights or legitimate interests of persons, there should, where appropriate, be a meaningful possibility of challenge and review.

The ability to challenge public action must not depend on social status, financial resources, or connections to government. It should form part of the ordinary structure of the legal system.

The possibility of challenge is one of the mechanisms through which legal limits on power become effective in practice.

1-6. Exceeding Legal Competence Must Be Subject to Effective Restraint §

If an institution exceeds its lawful competence, the legal system must provide mechanisms capable of stopping, correcting, suspending, or nullifying the unlawful action.

Depending on the nature of the violation, such mechanisms may include judicial review, annulment or suspension of a decision, mandatory correction, parliamentary oversight, independent inspection, or other lawful forms of institutional control.

A legal limit whose violation has no practical consequence is not an effective limit.

1-7. Exceptional Circumstances Must Not Create Unlimited Power §

Crises, war, states of emergency, or serious threats may require special public powers. But exceptional authority must not result in the indefinite suspension of the legal order.

Such powers must have a lawful basis, a defined purpose, a limited scope, a defined duration, and remain subject to oversight and review.

An exception must not become a permanent method of government.

1-8. Institutional Independence Does Not Mean Immunity from Legal Limits §

Some institutions require independence in order to perform their functions properly, including courts and certain oversight bodies.

Independence, however, does not mean unlimited power or immunity from accountability.

Institutional independence should protect against improper interference, not place an institution outside general legal rules, defined competences, or accountability mechanisms.

Test for Principle 1

To assess an institution or public authority, one may ask:

  • What is the lawful source of this authority?
  • Where are the limits of its competence defined?
  • Who can review the exercise of that authority?
  • How can a person affected by a decision challenge it?
  • If the institution exceeds its competence, what mechanism can restrain it?
  • Are exceptional powers limited in purpose and duration?

If these questions cannot be answered clearly, the risk of legitimate authority becoming unchecked power increases.

Conclusion

Public power is necessary for the governance of society, but the legitimacy of power does not arise from its being unlimited. Power becomes more legitimate and sustainable when its source is clear, its legal limits are defined in advance, its exercise is subject to effective oversight, its decisions can be challenged, and excesses of competence can be met with effective legal or institutional restraint.

Principle 1 of NAZM DAD

No power is absolute.

Legitimate power is power exercised within clearly defined legal limits and subject to effective oversight, review, and restraint.
