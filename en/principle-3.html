3. No Institution Is Beyond Accountability

Wherever public authority is exercised, there must be meaningful opportunities for questioning, independent review, and an effective response where wrongdoing is established.

Document ID: nazm-dad:p3:v1 | Version: 1.0 | Last revised: 2026-08-31

Public Authority Must Be Open to Questioning, Independent Review, and Effective Response

Any institution exercising authority over individual rights, public resources, or decisions that materially affect people's lives must be able to explain the basis of its decisions and actions and demonstrate that it acted within its lawful competence.

An institution's status, expertise, independence, or importance of mission cannot by itself exempt it from accountability.

Accountability does not weaken legitimate authority. It is one of the conditions of legitimacy, trustworthiness, and institutional durability.

3-1. Public Authority Without Accountability Lacks Sustainable Legitimacy Â§

Every institution entrusted with public authority must be accountable under law, to society, and to persons affected by its decisions.

At a minimum, accountability requires an institution to provide reasons for its decisions, permit meaningful questioning and review of its conduct, and allow an effective institutional or legal response where unlawful conduct, abuse of authority, or an excess of legal competence is established.

Accountability does not eliminate authority. It places authority within a legal structure capable of examining and correcting its exercise.

3-2. Accountability Is More Than Providing an Explanation Â§

Providing an explanation is one component of accountability, but explanation alone is insufficient.

A public decision should, to the extent compatible with law and the legitimate rights of others, be documented. Its legal and factual basis should be accessible and open to questioning.

Relevant evidence and reasoning should be reviewable, and where appropriate, challenge and correction should be possible.

An institution that merely explains a decision while remaining effectively immune from review, challenge, or correction is not fully accountable.

3-3. No Status, Mission, or Institutional Role Creates Complete Immunity from Accountability Â§

No government, parliament, court, security institution, executive body, oversight authority, or other institution exercising public authority should stand outside systems of accountability merely because of its mission or status.

The broader an institution's powers, the greater the resources under its control, or the more significant the effects of its decisions on individual rights, the stronger the corresponding need for proportionate accountability mechanisms.

Even institutions established to protect rights or oversee other institutions must themselves act within defined legal competences and legal rules.

3-4. Institutional Independence Does Not Mean Freedom from Accountability Â§

Some institutions require independence in order to perform their functions impartially, particularly institutions that must make decisions free from improper interference or oversee the exercise of power by others.

Independence, however, must not be confused with immunity from law or accountability.

Independence should prevent improper interference, while accountability ensures that the independent institution itself acts within defined legal competence, according to clear rules, and remains subject to appropriate review.

Independence and accountability are not opposites. Both may be necessary conditions of legitimate institutional action.

3-5. Oversight Must Be Independent, Effective, and Proportionate Â§

Oversight of an institution should not rest exclusively with that same institution or its direct subordinate bodies.

Depending on the nature of the institution and the public authority exercised, the legal order should provide an appropriate combination of independent oversight, institutional checks, and external review.

Effective oversight requires sufficient independence, lawful access to necessary information, authority to investigate, and the ability to follow through on findings.

An oversight body without effective means of oversight does not create genuine accountability.

3-6. Accountability Must Permit an Effective Institutional or Legal Response Where Wrongdoing Is Established Â§

Accountability becomes meaningful when an established violation of law, excess of legal competence, abuse of authority, or infringement of rights can lead to an effective response.

Depending on the nature of the violation, that response may include amendment or annulment of a decision, cessation of unlawful conduct, restoration of the prior situation, compensation, administrative or financial responsibility, or other proportionate corrective or legal responses provided by law.

Removal, suspension, or personal liability should likewise be imposed only where a lawful basis exists and the response is proportionate.

A response need not always be punitive. Its purpose may be to correct a decision, repair harm, restore legality, and reduce the risk of recurrence.

3-7. Oversight Institutions Must Themselves Be Accountable Â§

No oversight institution should fall outside systems of accountability merely because its function is to supervise others.

Oversight institutions must themselves operate within clearly defined legal competences, established procedures, duties to provide reasons, and appropriate mechanisms for review of their own conduct.

The precise form of accountability may differ in order to preserve institutional independence, but the principle of accountability must not disappear.

No institution that controls power should itself become an uncontrolled power.

3-8. Accountability Must Be Transparent, Timely, and Non-Discriminatory Â§

Accountability mechanisms should be as clear, understandable, and accessible as reasonably possible.

Review must not be rendered ineffective by unjustified delay, disproportionate cost, or unjustified discrimination.

Relevant parties should be able to present reasons and evidence under fair procedures, and the outcome of the review should, within lawful limits, be reasoned and understandable.

Effective accountability depends not only on the existence of review mechanisms, but also on their quality, reasonable timeliness, independence, and impartiality.

Test for Principle 3

To assess the accountability of an institution, one may ask:

  • Are the limits of its legal competence clear?
  • Are important decisions documented and reasoned?
  • Can affected persons question or challenge its decisions?
  • Is information necessary for meaningful review lawfully accessible?
  • Is there an independent body or effective mechanism capable of reviewing its conduct?
  • Are oversight institutions themselves subject to accountability?
  • If wrongdoing is established, can decisions be corrected or annulled, unlawful conduct stopped, and harm repaired?
  • Does the accountability process operate without discrimination and within a reasonable time?

If these questions cannot be answered clearly, the institution in question is not fully accountable, and the risk of unchecked authority increases.

Conclusion

Accountability is not the enemy of authority. It is a mechanism that keeps authority within its legitimate limits and makes the detection and correction of institutional error possible.

A trustworthy institution is not one that claims never to make mistakes. It is one whose decisions can be questioned and independently reviewed, and for which genuine mechanisms of correction and reparation exist when wrongdoing or error occurs.

Principle 3 of NAZM DAD

No institution is beyond accountability.

Wherever public authority is exercised, there must be meaningful opportunities for questioning, independent review, and an effective response where wrongdoing is established.
